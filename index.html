Welcome

Hi! I am Wei Lai (赖玮). I am an assistant professor in the Linguistics Department at California State University, Long Beach. My research interests lie at the intersection of phonetics, sociolinguistics, and psycholinguistics, including speech perception and production, sound change, and the social-cognitive mechanisms involved in processing sociolinguistic variation. I also have a long-standing interest in the production and perception of lexical tones and speech prosody.

I received my Ph.D. in Linguistics in 2021 from the University of Pennsylvania. The title of my doctoral dissertation is "The online adjustment of speaker-specific phonetic beliefs in multi-speaker speech perception". My dissertation advisor was Meredith Tamminga. After graduation, I continued my academic research as a postdoctoral scholar in Duane Watson's lab, in the Department of Psychology and Human Development at Vanderbilt University.

Before joining CSULB, I spent several years in industry as a language engineer on the Bedrock Data Team at Amazon Web Services. This experience informs my interest in teaching computational linguistics, advocating for linguists’ contributions to the tech industry, and advising students on navigating linguistic careers in big tech.

RESEARCH

I study the acoustic and perceptual aspects of speech, with a particular focus on speech variability. Using acoustic measurements of lab-elicited speech and speech corpora, I investigate how speakers encode linguistic information across different contexts and how listeners use social and contextual information to interpret variable speech signals. I also have a long-standing interest in suprasegmental aspects of speech, including pitch, timing, intensity, and voice quality, especially in lexical tones and speech prosody.

My main research areas are outlined below:

Integration of social-indexical cues in linguistic processing

In spoken language, linguistic meaning and information about a talker's social identity are closely interwoven. Listeners may find an interlocutor's speech more or less intelligible depending on their opinions of the speaker, and may hear different phonemes in the same ambiguous signal depending on the speaker's perceived identity. This work connects speech perception with social cognition: how is social-indexical information represented and associated with linguistic variants, and how do listeners use these associations to form expectations and interpret their interlocutors' speech?

Flexibility of speech perception: compensation, adaptation, generalization

The acoustic realization of a phoneme can vary substantially with the linguistic environment, acoustic surroundings, social context, speaking style, speech rate, talker identity, and lexical properties. Listeners can adjust their phoneme category boundaries in response to coarticulatory, phonotactic, lexical, and semantic contexts. They can also learn talker-specific phonetic distributions and generalize what they learn to new talkers. This research investigates the linguistic and social structures that shape perceptual compensation, adaptation, and generalization.

Acoustics and perception of lexical tone variation

Much research has examined the acoustics of tonal contrasts and the typology of tone systems, but tone variability remains less explored. Tone is primarily realized through fundamental frequency (F₀), which varies with a talker's sex and vocal state. My work investigates how talker sex, vocal effort, and tonal contrasts are jointly encoded in F₀, and how listeners resolve the ambiguity caused by acoustic overlap. I am also interested in how lexical and prosodic structures shape tonal variation, how to model that variation, and how neutral tones change over time across lexical items.

Measurement and perception of intonation and prosody

I am interested in the typology, phonologization, acoustics, perception, and modeling of intonation and prosody. One strand of my research examines how to measure prosodic parameters to reveal the underlying structure of prosody while separating it from unrelated sources of F₀ variation. Another uses perception to investigate how prosodic patterns give rise to contrasting interpretations of an utterance.
